Bali is broadly safe for tourists, and violent crime against visitors is uncommon relative to most Western cities. The risks that genuinely affect the largest number of travellers are more mundane: road traffic (especially scooter accidents), water conditions at certain beaches, and getting sick from food or water (commonly called Bali belly), not the kind of crime-driven safety concerns visitors sometimes worry about before a first trip.

That doesn't mean no precautions are needed. Petty theft (bag snatching, opportunistic theft from unlocked scooters or beach bags) does happen, particularly in busier tourist areas, and it's worth taking the same basic precautions you would in any popular tourist destination.

## The risks that actually matter most

Traffic is the single biggest practical safety issue in Bali for most visitors, particularly for those renting scooters without much local riding experience. Road conditions, traffic density and driving norms are different from many visitors' home countries, and scooter accidents are the most common serious incident tourists experience. See our guide on [renting a scooter in Bali](/blog/travel/scooter-rental-bali/) for how to reduce this risk if you plan to ride.

Water safety at certain beaches — particularly around rip currents on more exposed south and southwest-facing coasts — is a genuine risk worth respecting; swim at beaches with visible lifeguard presence where possible and heed any flag or warning systems in place. Bali belly, generally caused by unfamiliar bacteria in food or water rather than anything more serious, affects a meaningful share of visitors and is largely preventable with sensible food and water choices — see our dedicated guide on [avoiding Bali belly](/blog/travel/bali-belly-prevention/).

## Common safety concerns compared with actual risk levels

| Concern | Actual risk level | Practical response |
|---|---|---|
| Violent crime | Low relative to most Western cities | Standard travel awareness is sufficient |
| Petty theft (bags, valuables) | Moderate in busy tourist areas | Don't leave valuables visible or unattended |
| Traffic and scooter accidents | The most common serious risk | Consider your own riding experience honestly |
| Water safety (currents, unmarked hazards) | Real at certain beaches | Swim at lifeguarded beaches, respect warning flags |
| Food/water illness (Bali belly) | Common but largely preventable | Sensible food and water choices |
| Natural hazards (volcanic activity, occasional flooding) | Low but monitored by authorities | Follow local advisories if issued |

## Practical precautions worth taking

- Don't leave bags, phones or valuables visible or unattended on a scooter, at the beach or in a restaurant.
- Use registered transport (app-based rides or a known driver) rather than unmarked vehicles offering rides.
- Swim only at beaches with visible lifeguards or clear local knowledge of conditions, especially on more exposed coastlines.
- Be cautious with street food and tap water early in a trip while your system adjusts; build up gradually rather than avoiding local food entirely.
- Keep copies of important documents (passport, insurance) separate from the originals.

## Staying at a managed villa versus other accommodation

A private villa with its own pool and secure compound, particularly one professionally managed with staff on-site or on call, generally adds a layer of security and support compared with more open accommodation — someone to call if something goes wrong, a known, gated property rather than an unfamiliar area, and staff who know the local area well enough to advise on where's genuinely fine to walk at night and where isn't. This is part of why families in particular often prefer a managed villa over piecing together several types of accommodation on one trip.

## FAQ

### Is Bali safe for solo female travellers?

Bali is a well-established destination for solo female travellers, with a large existing community of long-term visitors and expats, and the same general precautions (awareness of surroundings, registered transport, caution with alcohol in unfamiliar settings) that apply to solo travel anywhere serve well here too.

### Is it safe to drink tap water in Bali?

No, tap water in Bali is not recommended for drinking; stick to bottled or filtered water, which is widely available, including typically provided at managed villas. This is one of the more preventable causes of getting unwell during a trip.

### Is Bali safe for families with young children?

Yes, Bali is a popular family destination, and a private villa with a secure, gated pool area is generally considered one of the safer accommodation choices for families with small children, provided normal pool supervision precautions are followed.

### Are there areas of Bali tourists should avoid?

Bali doesn't have widely recognised no-go areas for tourists in the way some destinations do; the more relevant precaution is general awareness in busy nightlife areas late at night and sensible caution around unfamiliar, poorly lit streets, as in any destination.

### What's the most common safety issue tourists actually experience in Bali?

Traffic-related incidents, particularly scooter accidents, are the most common serious safety issue visitors experience, followed by Bali belly from food or water. Both are largely preventable with sensible precautions rather than being inherent risks of visiting.
