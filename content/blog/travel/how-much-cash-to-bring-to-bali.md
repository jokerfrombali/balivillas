There is no single right figure, because the answer depends on how you plan to pay for things once you land, not on some fixed daily budget. For most travellers, arriving with a small amount of rupiah for the first taxi, a meal and tips, then drawing the rest from ATMs as needed, works better than carrying a large sum for the whole trip.

A workable starting point is enough rupiah to cover your airport transfer, your first day's food, and small tips, then let ATMs and your card handle the rest once you're settled. Bringing a stack of banknotes "just in case" mostly creates a security and exchange-rate problem you don't need.

## Why you don't need to carry a trip's worth of cash

Card acceptance has grown steadily across Bali's tourist areas, and ATMs are common in Sanur, Canggu, Umalas and near most of the Bukit. The practical constraint isn't availability of cash but convenience: withdrawing smaller amounts more often is usually cheaper and safer than one large withdrawal or bringing foreign banknotes to exchange.

If you're staying in one of our villas, the villa itself doesn't require cash on arrival — payment for your stay is arranged with us directly after your dates are confirmed, and nothing is charged automatically through the website. What you need cash for day to day is transport, food outside restaurants that take cards, market stalls, temple donations and staff tips.

## What actually needs cash versus card

Warungs (small local restaurants), traditional markets, temple entrance donations, most scooter rentals, and informal transport still lean heavily on cash. Larger restaurants, most cafés in Canggu and Seminyak, supermarkets, and hotel-style bookings usually take cards. Villa staff tips are almost always cash, since that's the practical way to hand something over directly.

## A practical way to plan the amount

Rather than picking an arbitrary total, work backwards from your trip length and how much you expect to spend on cash-only things: transport, tips, markets and temple visits. Multiply a rough daily cash figure by the number of days, add a buffer for the first day before you've located a good ATM, and treat the rest as card or ATM withdrawals.

This approach scales naturally with group size and trip length rather than assuming everyone needs the same fixed amount. A couple staying a week in a self-catered villa with a car and driver will use less day-to-day cash than a family relying on warungs and taxis for every meal.

## Bringing foreign currency versus withdrawing rupiah

Bringing US dollars or another major currency to exchange locally can work, but exchange rates and fees at airport kiosks are often worse than a bank ATM withdrawal once you land, and carrying foreign notes you then need to convert adds a step and a risk. If you do bring foreign cash, only exchange at licensed money changers (our guide on ATMs and money exchange in Bali covers how to spot a legitimate one) rather than informal street changers.

Many travellers find that skipping foreign cash altogether and relying on ATM withdrawals plus a card for larger purchases is simpler and avoids the double conversion of home currency to dollars and dollars to rupiah.

## Splitting cash across your group and your bags

For families or groups travelling together, it's worth splitting your cash rather than one person carrying the trip's whole supply. If a bag is lost or a wallet is misplaced, you still have access to funds through another person's card or cash. Keep a small emergency reserve separate from your daily spending money, ideally in the villa safe rather than on your person.

- Split cash between two or more people or bags, not one wallet
- Keep small notes for tips and warungs; large notes can be hard to break for small purchases
- Use the villa safe for anything beyond a day or two's spending money
- Note your bank's daily ATM withdrawal limit before you travel

## Currency declaration and carrying cash into Indonesia

If you plan to carry a large amount of cash into Indonesia, be aware that customs rules require declaration above a certain threshold (as of 2026, verify the current figure with imigrasi.go.id or Indonesian customs before you travel, since thresholds and enforcement can change). For most villa guests bringing a modest travel float, this won't be relevant, but anyone carrying a genuinely large sum for a property purchase or long stay should check the current rule directly rather than assume.

## What we see go wrong

The most common mistake is under-preparing for the first few hours: arriving late at night with no rupiah and finding the nearest ATM further away than expected. The second is over-preparing: carrying several days' cash in one place, which is simply more to lose or have stolen than most trips warrant. A middle path — a modest arrival float plus regular smaller withdrawals — avoids both problems.

Card decline notifications from your home bank flagging unusual overseas activity are also common; tell your bank you're travelling to Indonesia before you leave, so a legitimate ATM withdrawal or card payment isn't blocked at an inconvenient moment.

## What to do next

Bringing cash to Bali is less about a magic number and more about matching your cash plan to how you'll actually spend: transport, tips and markets need cash; most bigger purchases can go on a card. ## FAQ

### How much rupiah should I bring for a week in Bali?
Enough for your arrival transfer, a day or two of food and tips is usually sufficient as a starting float; most travellers then withdraw more from ATMs as needed rather than carrying a week's total from home. The right figure depends heavily on how much you plan to spend at cash-only places like warungs and markets.

### Can I use foreign cash like US dollars directly in Bali?
Some larger hotels and shops will accept US dollars informally, but it's not standard for everyday spending, and you'll typically get a worse rate than converting through a licensed money changer or an ATM withdrawal. It's simpler to treat foreign cash as something to exchange rather than spend directly.

### Is it safe to carry a lot of cash in Bali?
Carrying more cash than you need for a day or two increases the risk and impact of loss or theft with no real benefit, since ATMs and cards cover most needs. Keep spending cash on you and any larger reserve in the villa safe.

### Do I need cash for villa staff tips?
Yes, tipping is almost always handled in cash directly to staff rather than added to a bill. See our dedicated guide on tipping in Bali for typical practice and amounts to plan around.

### What happens if I run out of cash and the nearest ATM is far away?
This is uncommon in Sanur, Canggu, Umalas or Ungasan, where ATMs are reasonably common, but if you're staying somewhere remote it's worth asking your villa host or us in advance where the nearest reliable machine is. Keeping a card as backup for larger purchases avoids being stuck entirely without funds.
