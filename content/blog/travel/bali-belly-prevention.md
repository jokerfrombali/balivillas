Bali belly is the common name for the stomach upset — usually diarrhoea, cramping, sometimes nausea — that a meaningful share of visitors experience during a trip to Bali, most often from bacteria in water or food that a traveller's system isn't used to, rather than from any single specific cause. It's rarely serious and usually resolves within a few days with basic care, but it can meaningfully disrupt a trip, so prevention is worth taking seriously.

The good news is that most of the risk reduction comes down to a handful of specific, easy habits around water, food and hygiene, not avoiding Bali's food scene altogether, which would be a genuine loss on a trip here.

## Water: the single biggest factor

Never drink tap water in Bali, including for brushing teeth if you want to be thorough, and be cautious with ice in places you're unsure about, since ice made from tap water carries the same risk. Bottled or filtered water is widely available and inexpensive; most managed villas, including ours, provide filtered or bottled water as standard, so check this is available at your accommodation rather than assuming.

## Food: sensible precautions, not blanket avoidance

Freshly cooked, hot food from busy places (locals eating there is a genuinely useful signal of turnover and freshness) is generally lower risk than food that's been sitting out or reheated. Peel fruit yourself rather than eating pre-cut fruit from an unfamiliar source, and be a little more cautious with raw vegetables and salads in the first few days of a trip while your system adjusts, especially from less established places.

Street food and warungs are not inherently riskier than restaurants — busy, high-turnover places of either kind are generally safer than quiet ones, since food isn't sitting around. Use your judgement on cleanliness and turnover rather than avoiding a whole category of food.

## Comparing common risk factors

| Source | Relative risk | Practical approach |
|---|---|---|
| Tap water (drinking, ice) | High | Always use bottled or filtered water |
| Freshly cooked hot food, busy places | Lower | Generally fine, a good default choice |
| Pre-cut fruit, salads from unfamiliar sources | Moderate | Peel your own fruit, be cautious early in the trip |
| Food from quiet, low-turnover places | Higher | Prefer busy places with visible turnover |
| Unwashed hands before eating | Moderate, easily controlled | Carry hand sanitiser, wash hands regularly |

## Building up gradually rather than avoiding local food

A common and effective approach is easing in during the first day or two — sticking to well-reviewed restaurants and familiar-style food — before branching out to more local warungs and street food once your system has had a little time to adjust. This isn't strictly necessary for everyone, but it reduces risk for travellers whose gut is more sensitive to unfamiliar bacteria, without missing out on Bali's food scene for the whole trip.

## What to pack and have on hand

- Oral rehydration salts (widely available in pharmacies in Bali too, but useful to have on arrival).
- A basic anti-diarrhoeal medication, used sensibly and not as a first response to mild symptoms.
- Hand sanitiser for use before eating, especially when soap and water aren't immediately available.
- Your travel insurance details, in case symptoms are severe enough to need medical attention.

## If it happens anyway

Most cases resolve within a few days with rest, hydration (oral rehydration salts are more effective than water alone for replacing lost electrolytes) and a bland diet as symptoms ease. Stay especially attentive to hydration with children, since dehydration develops faster in kids. Seek medical attention if symptoms are severe, include a high fever, blood, or don't improve after a few days — Bali has good private medical clinics in the main tourist areas that are used to treating exactly this. Check your [travel insurance](/blog/travel/bali-travel-insurance/) covers medical treatment before you travel, so this isn't a decision complicated by cost if it happens.

## What to do next

A few consistent habits — bottled water always, sensible food choices, hand hygiene, and a gradual approach in the first couple of days — prevent most cases of Bali belly without limiting what you eat and where you go on the trip. Pack basic rehydration and anti-diarrhoeal supplies just in case, and know where the nearest reliable clinic is relative to your accommodation.

## FAQ

### Is it safe to eat street food in Bali?

Yes, generally — busy, high-turnover street food stalls are often as safe as, or safer than, quiet restaurants, since food isn't sitting around for long. Use visible turnover and cleanliness as your guide rather than avoiding street food as a category.

### Can I brush my teeth with tap water in Bali?

It's safer to use bottled or filtered water for brushing teeth too, particularly early in a trip, since even small amounts of unfamiliar bacteria can trigger symptoms in a sensitive gut.

### How long does Bali belly usually last?

Most cases resolve within two to four days with rest, hydration and a bland diet. If symptoms are severe, include a high fever or blood, or persist beyond a few days, seek medical attention rather than waiting it out.

### Do villas in Bali provide safe drinking water?

Most managed villas, including ours, provide filtered or bottled water as standard, but it's worth confirming this specifically with whichever villa or accommodation you're booking rather than assuming.

### Should I take probiotics before travelling to Bali to prevent Bali belly?

Some travellers find starting probiotics a week or two before a trip helpful, though evidence varies by individual, and it's not a substitute for basic water and food precautions, which make the biggest difference regardless.
