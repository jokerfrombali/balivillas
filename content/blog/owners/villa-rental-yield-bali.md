Bali villa rental yield is usually quoted as gross yield — annual rental income divided by purchase price — but gross yield tells you almost nothing about what an owner actually keeps, because it ignores management commission, maintenance, staff, utilities, licensing and OTA fees. Net yield, after those costs, is typically several percentage points lower than the gross figure agents quote, and it's the number that actually matters for comparing two properties or two management offers.

This guide sets out how to calculate both figures properly, what drives the gap between them, and how to read a yield claim from an agent or developer without taking the headline number at face value.

## Gross yield is a starting point, not an answer

Gross yield is straightforward to calculate: take a realistic annual rental income figure and divide it by the purchase price, then multiply by 100. The problem is that "realistic annual rental income" is doing a lot of work in that sentence, and it's the number most likely to be optimistic in a sales pitch. A villa marketed with a high gross yield based on peak-season nightly rates applied across 365 days will never actually achieve that figure once low season, vacancy and turnover gaps are accounted for.

Ask any yield claim two questions: what occupancy rate is it assuming, and is the nightly rate used a realistic year-round average or a high-season snapshot. Neither of us can give you a reliable occupancy or income figure for a specific villa without seeing the property and its history — treat any generic percentage quoted without that context as marketing rather than analysis.

## What separates gross from net

| Deduction | Typical impact |
|---|---|
| Management commission | A percentage of actual bookings, varies by manager and scope |
| OTA commission (Airbnb, Booking.com) | Separate from management fee, usually a fixed platform percentage |
| Housekeeping and maintenance | Ongoing, plus periodic repairs and replacements |
| Staff wages (if villa-specific staff) | Recurring, regardless of occupancy |
| Utilities during vacant periods | Owner cost even when the villa isn't earning |
| Licensing, tax and compliance | One-off setup plus recurring tax on rental income |

Net yield is what remains after all of that, expressed as a percentage of purchase price. It's the figure worth comparing across properties, not gross. ## Rental income and sale price move together, which distorts the picture

An area with strong rental demand also tends to have higher purchase prices, because buyers bid up properties with a track record of good bookings. That means a headline yield percentage can look similar across very different villas — a cheaper property with modest but steady income, and an expensive one with strong income but a price that's already priced in the demand. Comparing yield alone, without also comparing the absolute numbers and the area's price trajectory, misses that a lower-yield property in a rising area can still be the better long-term hold.

## Occupancy assumptions matter more than the nightly rate

A villa quoting a high nightly rate but low realistic occupancy can underperform one with a modest rate and steady bookings. Since neither of us should present an occupancy figure as fact, the practical approach is to ask for booking pace data — actual nights booked over the last 12 months, not a projection — from any manager or agent making a yield claim. If they can't produce it, treat the yield quoted as unverified.

- Ask for 12 months of actual booked nights, not a projected occupancy percentage
- Ask what nightly rate was used to calculate the income figure, and over what season
- Ask for the full list of deductions used to get from gross to net
- Compare the net figure, not the gross headline, across any properties you're weighing

## Tax and legal factors that affect net yield

Rental income in Indonesia is subject to tax, and the applicable rate and any regional tourism levy should be confirmed with a licensed tax adviser rather than assumed from a blog post (as of 2026, verify with a licensed adviser, since rules have been subject to change). A villa operating without the correct tourism licence or business registration carries compliance risk that can affect both income and resale value, which is a separate cost that rarely appears in a yield calculation but should factor into any real comparison.

## A worked example: two villas, same headline yield

Villa A costs less to buy and generates modest but steady income; Villa B costs considerably more but sits in an area with stronger, more consistent demand, and both are quoted at a similar gross yield by their respective agents. On the surface they look equivalent. Once you net out costs, the picture usually changes: Villa B's higher absolute income may support a management commission and staffing structure more efficiently (fixed costs like a licensing renewal or a maintenance contract matter less as a share of a bigger income base), while Villa A's modest income has to absorb the same fixed costs from a smaller base, which can pull its net yield down further than the headline gross figure suggested.

This is why comparing net yield, and the absolute numbers behind it, matters more than comparing two percentages side by side. A lower gross yield on a more expensive, higher-demand property can still be the financially stronger choice once real costs and growth trajectory are factored in.

## A simple checklist for testing any yield claim

1. Ask for 12 months of actual booked nights for the specific property, not a projected occupancy percentage
2. Confirm whether the nightly rate used in the calculation is a realistic year-round average or a high-season figure
3. Get the full list of deductions used to go from gross to net, not just a management commission line
4. Compare net yield and absolute net income, not just the headline gross percentage
5. Check whether the villa's licensing and tax position is current, since a compliance gap is a cost that rarely appears in a yield figure but can affect both income and resale

Working through this checklist before accepting any quoted yield is the single most useful habit for evaluating a rental villa purchase or reassessing one you already own.

## Why yield alone shouldn't be the only decision factor

Yield is a useful comparison tool, but it's not the whole picture for most owners. Capital appreciation potential, personal use of the villa, ease of eventual resale, and how much active involvement you want in running it all matter alongside the percentage. A villa with a slightly lower net yield but stronger long-term appreciation potential, or one that suits your own holiday use well, can be the better choice even if a pure yield comparison would point elsewhere. Treat yield as one input into a broader decision, not the entire answer on its own.

## FAQ

### What's a realistic Bali villa rental yield?

There's no single reliable industry-wide figure, and any specific percentage quoted without seeing a property's actual booking history should be treated as marketing. Ask for real occupancy data and calculate net yield yourself using the deduction categories above rather than accepting a headline number.

### Does gross or net yield matter more when comparing villas?

Net yield, because it reflects what an owner actually keeps after management, maintenance, OTA fees, staff and tax. Two villas with the same gross yield can differ significantly in net once their cost structures are compared properly.

### How does management commission affect rental yield?

Commission is one of several deductions between gross and net yield, and it's usually charged as a percentage of actual bookings rather than a flat fee. See [OTA commission for Bali villas](/blog/owners/ota-commission-comparison-bali/) for how commission and platform fees interact and affect the final number.

### Why do villa sale prices and rental yields seem to move together?

Areas with proven rental demand attract higher purchase prices, since buyers factor in the income track record. That can make yield percentages look similar across very different properties, which is why comparing absolute income and price trajectory matters alongside the percentage.
