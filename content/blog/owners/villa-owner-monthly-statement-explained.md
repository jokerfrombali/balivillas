A villa owner monthly statement in Bali worth trusting has a consistent structure: a booking log for the period, gross revenue per booking, commission deducted and how it was calculated, itemised expenses, and a net payout figure that should match what actually lands in your bank account. If any section is missing or vague, that's the specific thing to ask your manager to fix.

Knowing how to read this statement properly — not just glance at the bottom line — is what turns monthly reporting from a formality into a genuinely useful tool for tracking your villa's performance.

## The booking log

This section lists every stay during the reporting period: check-in and check-out dates, booking channel (direct, Airbnb, Booking.com or other), and the length of stay. Cross-check this against your own awareness of the villa's calendar if you have any visibility into it — a booking log that doesn't match what you'd expect, in either direction, is worth asking about.

Longer stays and direct bookings typically carry different commission economics than short OTA stays, so this section is also where you start understanding your villa's actual booking mix, not just the total.

## Gross revenue per booking

Gross revenue is the full amount the guest paid before any deductions — commission, OTA fees, or payment processing charges. This figure should be shown per booking, not only as a monthly total, so you can spot whether any individual booking looks unusually low or high relative to your villa's typical rates for that season.

| Statement section | What it shows | What to check |
|---|---|---|
| Booking log | Dates, channel, length of stay | Matches your own calendar awareness |
| Gross revenue | Full booking value before deductions | No bookings missing or unexplained |
| Commission | Rate and base used, per booking | Matches your contracted rate |
| Expenses | Itemised, with brief description | No vague "miscellaneous" lump sums |
| Net payout | Final amount due to you | Matches your bank deposit exactly |

## Commission: the section worth scrutinising most

The commission line should state clearly whether it's calculated on gross booking value or net of OTA commission and payment fees, matching whatever your contract specifies. If your statement shows commission as a single deducted figure without showing the calculation, ask for it to be broken out — this is the section most likely to contain a billing error, and the hardest one to catch without the underlying calculation shown.

For a deeper look at how commission structures typically work and what the difference between gross and net actually means for your payout, see [villa management contract clauses](/blog/owners/villa-management-contract-clauses/), which covers the commission clause specifically.

## Expenses: the section that needs real detail

Expenses should be itemised individually — a specific maintenance call-out, a laundry cost, a licensing renewal fee — rather than bundled into one line. A statement showing a single "expenses: $X" line with no breakdown makes it impossible to verify whether the spending was reasonable or matches what you'd separately approved, if your contract requires approval above a certain threshold.

- Ask for a brief description with every expense line, not just an amount
- Cross-check any expense above your contract's approval threshold against your own approval record
- Flag recurring expense categories that seem to be growing month to month without explanation

## Occupancy context

Many well-structured statements include a brief occupancy summary — nights booked against nights available for the period — even though this isn't strictly a financial line item. This context helps you interpret the revenue figures: a lower gross revenue month makes more sense if occupancy was genuinely lower, versus if occupancy held steady but revenue per booking dropped, which points to a pricing question instead.

## The net payout and reconciliation

The final figure should match, to the cent, what actually arrives in your bank account, adjusted only for any currency conversion or transfer fees your manager has separately disclosed. If the statement's net figure and your bank deposit don't match and there's no explanation, that's worth raising immediately — a persistent, unexplained mismatch is a bigger red flag than almost anything else on the statement.

## A simple monthly review routine

1. Check the booking log against your own calendar awareness, if any
2. Verify the commission calculation matches your contracted rate and base
3. Review itemised expenses for anything unexplained or unusually large
4. Confirm the net payout figure matches your bank deposit
5. Note any occupancy or pricing trend worth raising with your manager

Doing this consistently, even briefly, each month is what catches small errors before they compound across a year.

## Comparing two statements side by side

If you're weighing up two management offers, ask both for a sample statement and lay them next to each other. The comparison usually reveals more than either company's sales pitch: one might show commission calculated per booking against a clearly stated base, while the other bundles a single deduction with no working shown. One might itemise every expense with a one-line description, while the other lists a single monthly "operating costs" figure. Neither format is necessarily dishonest, but the itemised version is the one you can actually audit, and that difference matters more over a full year of statements than a slightly lower headline commission rate.

| What to compare | Itemised statement | Vague statement |
|---|---|---|
| Commission | Shown per booking with base and rate | One deducted figure, no calculation shown |
| Expenses | Individual line items with description | Single lump "expenses" total |
| Occupancy | Nights booked vs available included | Not mentioned |
| Net payout | Reconciles exactly to bank deposit | Approximate, or unexplained gaps |

## What goes wrong when owners only check the bottom line

The most common mistake we see is an owner glancing at the net payout figure each month, seeing it's roughly what they expected, and filing the statement away without reading the detail. That habit works fine until the month it doesn't — a miscalculated commission rate, a duplicated expense, or a booking that was never recorded can sit unnoticed for months if nobody checks the underlying lines, because the net figure alone can still look plausible even with an error buried inside it.

The fix costs a few minutes a month: run through the five-point routine above rather than just the final number, and raise anything that doesn't add up while it's a small discrepancy rather than a pattern spanning several statements.

## Keeping your own records alongside the statement

Even with a trustworthy manager, it's worth keeping your own simple record of each month's key figures — gross revenue, commission, net payout — separate from whatever your manager stores. This matters for two practical reasons: it gives you continuous records if you ever switch managers and need historical data that a departing company may be slow to hand over, and it makes year-over-year comparisons easier to run yourself rather than digging back through old statements each time you want to check a trend. A simple spreadsheet with one row per month is enough; it doesn't need to duplicate the full statement, just the figures you'd want on hand quickly.

## FAQ

### What's the difference between gross revenue and net payout on a villa owner statement?

Gross revenue is the full amount a guest paid before any deductions; net payout is what actually lands in your account after commission, expenses and any fees are subtracted. A transparent statement shows both figures clearly, along with the calculation connecting them, for every booking.

### Why doesn't my net payout always match the statement figure exactly?

This usually comes down to currency conversion or bank transfer fees if you're being paid to an overseas account, which some managers disclose separately from the statement itself. Ask your manager to confirm whether any such fees apply and to note them explicitly on future statements if they aren't already.

### Should expenses be listed individually or as one total on my monthly statement?

Individually, with a brief description for each one. A single lump "expenses" line makes it impossible to verify whether spending was reasonable or matches anything you separately approved, so ask for itemisation if your current statement doesn't already provide it.

### How do I know if my commission is being calculated correctly on my statement?

The statement should show the calculation explicitly per booking — the gross or net base used and the rate applied — not just a final deducted figure. Compare this against your contract's commission clause, and ask your manager to explain any discrepancy you can't reconcile yourself.

### What should I do if my monthly statement is consistently late or incomplete?

Raise it directly and specifically, asking for the exact structure described above rather than a general complaint. If the issue persists after a direct request, treat it as a signal about the broader relationship, not just the reporting itself, and consider whether it's time to review your options.
