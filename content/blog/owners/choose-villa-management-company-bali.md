Choosing a villa management company in Bali means checking five things in order: what the service actually covers, how the fee is structured, whether the company has a clean compliance track record, how they report to owners, and what happens if you want to leave. Skip any of these and you tend to find out the hard way, usually during a slow month or a maintenance dispute.

This is a process, not a gut call based on a glossy pitch. Below is the sequence we'd recommend running through with every company on your shortlist, in the order that surfaces problems earliest.

## Step 1: Confirm what's actually in scope

Before comparing fees, get a written list of what each company does day to day — guest communication, pricing and calendar management, housekeeping, maintenance coordination, staff supervision, and reporting. Companies calling themselves villa managers cover very different amounts of ground; some are closer to a booking agent. [What does a villa manager do](/blog/owners/what-does-a-villa-manager-do/) sets out the full list of functions to check against, so use that as your baseline before you start comparing quotes.

## Step 2: Understand the fee structure, not just the number

Get the commission clause in writing and ask exactly what it's calculated on — gross booking value or net of OTA and processing fees — since the same percentage can mean different money depending on the base. Ask what's included in the commission and what gets billed separately, such as maintenance, marketing or licensing setup.

| Question | Why it matters |
|---|---|
| Gross or net commission base? | Same rate, different real cost |
| What's inside the fee vs billed separately? | Headline rate can mislead |
| Fixed fee, commission, or hybrid? | Changes who carries the downside risk |
| How are repairs above a threshold approved? | Avoids surprise invoices |

We ask for terms case by case rather than publishing one rate, because a villa's size, location and condition change what's realistic — the same approach any manager worth using should take.

## Step 3: Check compliance and legal footing

Ask which licences the company holds or helps arrange for your villa — a pondok wisata or equivalent tourism permit, NIB business registration, and NPWP tax registration — and whether they can show you evidence for an existing managed villa. Renting short-term without the right licensing carries real risk, and a manager who's vague on this point is a warning sign, not a minor gap.

Tax on rental income also applies (as of 2026, verify current rates and any regional tourist levy with a licensed adviser, since rules have shifted recently), and a competent manager should be able to explain, in plain terms, how that affects an owner's net return without pretending to give tax advice themselves.

## Step 4: Test their reporting before you sign

Ask for a sample of the monthly report an existing owner client receives — with identifying details removed if needed. A report that clearly shows bookings, revenue, expenses and any pending decisions tells you more about a company's operational discipline than any sales conversation will. If a company can't produce one, or the sample is vague, treat that as informative.

## Step 5: Read the exit terms before the entry terms

It's tempting to focus entirely on onboarding, but the contract clause that matters most if things go wrong is the exit clause: notice period, handover process, and what happens to bookings already confirmed for dates after you leave. Clear exit terms are a sign of a company confident in its own service; vague ones are a sign to negotiate harder or walk away.

## Questions worth asking directly

Once you've narrowed the field, a live conversation is where the real gaps show up — vague answers here matter more than a polished proposal document. ### Weighing area specialists against island-wide operators

Some management companies focus tightly on one part of Bali — a handful of villas in Sanur, say, or a cluster in Canggu — while others operate across several areas. Neither approach is automatically better, but each carries a different trade-off worth weighing against your villa's location. An area specialist tends to know the local guest profile, the going rates for comparable villas nearby, and the practical logistics — staff availability, supplier relationships, typical maintenance issues for that area's building styles — in more depth than a company spread thinly across the island.

An operator managing villas across multiple areas, on the other hand, often has more established internal systems, a bigger maintenance team to draw on, and more resilience if one staff member is unavailable. We manage villas across Sanur, Canggu, Umalas and Ungasan, each with a distinct guest profile — Sanur skews toward families and longer stays, Canggu toward surf and digital nomad guests, Umalas sits quietly between the two, and Ungasan's clifftop villas draw a different kind of traveller again — so ask any company you're considering how they tailor their approach across areas rather than running one generic playbook everywhere.

### Trusting first impressions less than the paperwork

A confident sales conversation is not evidence of operational competence, and it's worth deliberately separating the two when you're evaluating a shortlist. The company that answers every question smoothly in a call isn't necessarily the one with the cleanest licensing status or the most honest sample report — sometimes it's the opposite, because a company less polished in sales conversations is putting more resource into actual operations.

Weight the paperwork — the written scope, the fee clause, the licensing evidence, the sample report — more heavily than how the conversation felt. If a company's answers in writing don't match what was said verbally, treat the written version as the one that counts, and ask for clarification on the gap before moving forward.

This is also a good moment to ask how long the company has managed villas in Bali specifically, and to request contact with a current owner client if they're able to arrange it. A company willing to put you in touch with someone they already work with has less to hide than one that deflects the request.

## FAQ

### Is there a single "best" villa management company in Bali?

No — the right company depends on your villa's location, size and your own involvement preferences, not a universal ranking. Run the same five-step process against your shortlist rather than looking for a general best-of list, since scope and fit vary enormously between properties.

### How many villa management companies should I compare before deciding?

Three is usually enough to see real variation in scope, fee structure and reporting quality without the process dragging on for months. Comparing only one company means you have nothing to benchmark their answers against.

### Should I choose a company that only manages a few villas or a larger operator?

Both have trade-offs: a smaller operator may offer more personal attention, while a larger one may have more established systems and staff depth. Ask how many villas the company currently manages and in which areas, and weigh that against how hands-on you want the relationship to be.

### What's a red flag when interviewing a villa management company?

Vagueness on licensing status, an unwillingness to share a sample report, and a fee structure that's hard to pin down in writing are the three most common warning signs. A company confident in its service is usually happy to be specific on all three.

### Can I switch villa management companies later if I'm not happy?

Yes, though the process depends on your contract's exit terms — notice period, handover of bookings already confirmed, and any licensing that's registered under the outgoing manager. Check exit terms at signing so switching later isn't harder than it needs to be.
