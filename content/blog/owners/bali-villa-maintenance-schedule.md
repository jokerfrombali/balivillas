A Bali villa maintenance schedule needs to cover four streams on different cycles: pool (daily or near-daily), garden (regular, several times a week), aircon and equipment (quarterly-ish servicing), and the building itself (periodic inspection for humidity-related wear). Tracking these together, rather than leaving each to a different contractor's own memory, is what keeps a villa from developing expensive, avoidable problems.

This guide lays out a practical schedule structure, why Bali's climate makes some of this more urgent than in drier climates, and how to keep the schedule from quietly slipping.

## Why Bali's climate changes the maintenance calculus

Tropical humidity, heavy rain during wet season, and constant sun exposure accelerate wear on materials and equipment faster than a temperate climate would. Wood swells and needs re-treating more often, metal fittings corrode faster near the coast, mould develops quickly in poorly ventilated spaces, and aircon units work harder and need more frequent servicing than in cooler climates. A maintenance schedule designed around a temperate-climate assumption will under-service a Bali villa.

## A practical schedule framework

| Stream | Frequency | Core tasks |
|---|---|---|
| Pool | Daily to near-daily | Chemical balance, skimming, equipment check |
| Garden | Several times weekly | Mowing, pruning, weeding, irrigation check |
| Aircon | Quarterly | Filter cleaning, gas level check, coil cleaning |
| Appliances | As needed plus annual check | Kitchen equipment, water heaters, washing machines |
| Building fabric | Every 6–12 months | Roof, gutters, timber, paint, waterproofing checks |
| Pest control | Regular, area-dependent | Termite and general pest prevention |

This is a starting framework, not a fixed rule — a villa closer to the coast, with more timber, or with a larger pool needs a tighter schedule on the relevant lines than a smaller, simpler property.

## Preventive vs reactive maintenance

The core distinction that separates well-run villas from poorly-run ones is preventive maintenance on a schedule versus reactive maintenance only after something visibly breaks. Reactive maintenance is always more expensive in the end — a small roof leak caught early costs a fraction of what water damage to ceilings and walls costs once it's been dripping unnoticed for months. A schedule exists specifically to catch small problems before they compound.

Aircon servicing deserves particular focus given how hard units work in Bali's climate and how directly they affect guest comfort — a broken aircon mid-stay is one of the fastest routes to a bad review. ## Tracking the schedule so it actually happens

A written schedule that nobody checks against is not meaningfully different from no schedule. What makes a maintenance schedule work in practice:

- A simple log — date, task, who did it — kept centrally rather than in each contractor's head
- A named person responsible for checking the log is being kept, not just for doing the work
- Photo documentation for less frequent tasks (roof, building fabric checks) to compare condition over time
- A monthly summary to the owner covering what was done, useful even for owners not asking for it explicitly

This overlaps directly with the reporting an owner should expect from any manager — a schedule with no visibility to the owner is hard to verify is actually being followed.

## Seasonal adjustments worth building in

Wet season (roughly November through March) increases urgency around roof and drainage checks, mould prevention and garden growth management, while dry season shifts pressure toward aircon load and pool evaporation. A schedule that treats every month identically misses this seasonal shift, which is a reasonable adjustment to build in rather than something to handle reactively when the season changes.

## FAQ

### How often should a villa's roof and gutters be checked?

Every six to twelve months is a reasonable baseline, with more frequent checks during and just after wet season when heavy rain puts the most stress on drainage and roofing. A missed leak can cause disproportionate damage in Bali's humidity, so this shouldn't be left to a multi-year cycle.

### Does a newer villa need less maintenance than an older one?

Less initially, but not none — Bali's climate stresses materials and equipment regardless of age, and a new villa still needs the same preventive schedule, just with a lower likelihood of finding problems in the early years. Deferring maintenance because a villa is new is a common and avoidable mistake.

### Who should be responsible for keeping the maintenance schedule?

Ideally one named person or manager, not a collection of independent contractors each managing their own piece without central visibility. Without a single point of accountability, gaps between different contractors' schedules are where problems slip through unnoticed.

### How does wet season change the maintenance schedule?

Wet season increases urgency around roof, gutter and drainage checks, and around mould prevention in less ventilated spaces, while garden growth also accelerates and needs more frequent attention. Dry season shifts the emphasis toward aircon load and pool water evaporation instead.

### Should maintenance records be shared with guests or only the owner?

Records are primarily for the owner and manager to track condition and catch problems early — they're not typically guest-facing. What guests experience is the result of the schedule being followed, not the schedule itself, which is why consistent upkeep matters more than documenting it for marketing purposes.
