Nyepi is Bali's Day of Silence, a Hindu New Year observance during which the whole island effectively stops for 24 hours: no flights in or out, streets empty, lights kept low, and most tourism services closed. Staying in a private villa is one of the more comfortable ways to experience it, because you spend the day inside your own compound rather than confined to a single hotel room.

The date moves each year with the Balinese lunar calendar, and the exact scope of restrictions (internet access, for example) has varied between recent years and can differ slightly by district. Anyone with a stay overlapping Nyepi should check the confirmed date and current rules close to arrival, either with us or with official sources, rather than relying on a fixed date carried over from a previous year.

## What actually shuts down

On Nyepi itself, expect the following, broadly consistent across Bali:

- **The airport closes.** Ngurah Rai International Airport typically suspends all flights for the full day, so no arrivals or departures are possible; anyone flying in or out needs to plan dates carefully around it.
- **Streets are empty.** Pecalang (local traditional security) patrol villages and enforce staying off the roads; even walking outside your villa compound is generally restricted, with narrow exceptions for genuine emergencies.
- **Shops, restaurants and most services close.** Convenience stores, warungs, spas and tour operators shut for the day; villa staff typically also observe the day, so plan meals and supplies in advance.
- **Lights are kept low or off at night**, out of respect for the observance, and this is usually followed inside villas too.
- **Internet and mobile signal** have in some recent years been restricted or slowed in parts of Bali as part of the observance, though the scope has varied by year; check current guidance rather than assuming full connectivity (as of 2026, verify).

## What a day in the villa actually looks like

Guests who have stayed with us through Nyepi describe it as a quiet, unusually calm day: pool time, reading, board games, and an early night. Because you cannot leave the compound, the villa's own facilities matter more than usual — a private pool, a garden, a kitchen stocked in advance, and (where possible) downloaded entertainment rather than relying on streaming, given that connectivity can be reduced. It is worth treating the day as a deliberate pause rather than something to work around; many guests say it becomes one of the more memorable parts of their trip.

## Stocking up before the day

Because shops and villa staff largely pause for Nyepi, plan meals and supplies the day before:

1. Do a full grocery shop the day before Nyepi, not the morning of — see our guide on [grocery delivery to your villa](/blog/renting/grocery-delivery-to-your-villa/) for how to arrange this ahead of time.
2. Confirm with your villa host whether housekeeping or a caretaker will be available at all, even for emergencies.
3. Charge devices and download any films, books or games you might want, in case connectivity is reduced.
4. Buy any medication or essentials you might need for the following two days as well, since some services take a day or two to fully resume afterwards.
5. Tell anyone expecting to reach you that you may be unreachable or slow to respond for the day.

## The day before and the day after

The days immediately around Nyepi are not quiet in the same way. The evening before typically features Ogoh-ogoh parades, large papier-mâché effigies paraded through villages, which are worth seeing if your villa's area holds one nearby. The day after, called Ngembak Geni, sees the island gradually return to normal, though some services, particularly drivers and tour operators, may still be limited as everyone resumes work.

## Areas and how they differ

The core rules of Nyepi (no travel, no lights, quiet) apply island-wide, but how strictly they are enforced and how a village organises the evening before can vary slightly by area. Villas in quieter areas like Sanur or Ungasan tend to feel naturally suited to a still day; Canggu and Umalas, usually busier, go unusually quiet, which some guests find a striking contrast. ## Booking dates around Nyepi

Because Nyepi's date shifts each year with the lunar calendar, always check the confirmed date for your specific travel year before locking in flights, ideally through an official airline or airport source rather than a date carried over from a previous year's itinerary. If your trip spans the day either side, expect flight schedules to compress in the days immediately before and after, as airlines cluster arrivals and departures around the closure; book those adjacent days earlier than you otherwise might, since seats fill quickly.

## Travelling with children during Nyepi

Families with young children sometimes worry that a full day confined to the villa will be difficult, but most find it easier than expected: a private pool, garden space to run around in, and no pressure to be anywhere makes it closer to a lazy day at home than a restrictive one. Pack extra activities specifically for this day, since it is the one day of the trip where a spontaneous outing genuinely is not an option, and treat it as a planned rest day within a longer itinerary rather than a day to fill with excursions.

## Working remotely around Nyepi

Digital nomads and remote workers who normally rely on villa Wi-Fi for daily work should treat Nyepi as a full day off, both because connectivity may be reduced and because it is, practically and culturally, a day meant for stillness rather than productivity. If you have a genuinely unmovable deadline or meeting, plan it for the day before or after rather than assuming you can work through Nyepi itself; most long-term residents build this into their calendar the same way they would plan around a public holiday anywhere else in the world.

## Guests who choose to time a trip around it deliberately

Not every guest treats Nyepi as something to plan around reluctantly. Some travellers deliberately time a Bali trip to include it, drawn by the contrast between the usual pace of the island and a single enforced day of total stillness, or by wanting to see the Ogoh-ogoh parades the evening before. ## FAQ

### Does the airport close on Nyepi?

Yes, Ngurah Rai International Airport typically closes fully for the day, with no arrivals or departures possible. Check flight schedules carefully once the exact date for your travel year is confirmed, since this affects both arriving and departing guests.

### Can I leave my villa during Nyepi?

Generally no. Pecalang enforce staying off the streets across the island, with narrow exceptions for genuine emergencies. Plan to spend the full day inside your villa compound, which is why having a private pool and garden makes the day far more comfortable.

### Will I have internet during Nyepi?

Connectivity has been restricted or slowed in parts of Bali during Nyepi in some recent years, though the scope has varied by year and location. Check current guidance closer to your travel date rather than assuming full access, and download anything you might want in advance.

### Are shops and restaurants open during Nyepi?

No, almost all shops, restaurants and services close for the full day, and villa staff typically also observe it. Do a full grocery shop the day before, and confirm with your host what support, if any, is available during the observance itself.

### What happens the evening before and the day after Nyepi?

The evening before often features Ogoh-ogoh parades with large parade effigies, worth watching if your area holds one nearby. The day after, called Ngembak Geni, sees the island gradually resume normal activity, though some services may still be limited for a day or so.

### Is Nyepi a good time to stay in a villa in Bali?

Many guests find it one of the more memorable parts of their trip, since the enforced quiet is unusual and a private pool villa is a comfortable place to experience it. Planning ahead for supplies, flights and entertainment makes the difference between a smooth day and a frustrating one.
