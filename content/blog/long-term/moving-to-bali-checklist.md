A moving to Bali checklist works best split into two stages: what needs sorting before you leave home, and what's better handled once you've actually landed and can see the place for yourself. Trying to lock down everything in advance, especially accommodation for a full year, before you've spent any time here in person, is a common and avoidable mistake.

This checklist covers the practical mechanics of the move; if you're moving with children specifically, see our guide on moving to Bali with kids for family-specific considerations.

## Before you leave: what to sort from home

- **Visa.** Confirm which visa category fits your intended stay length and purpose, ideally with a licensed agent, well before booking flights. See our guide on what visa you need to rent a villa in Bali.
- **Finances.** Set up a way to access money in Indonesia (international cards work widely, but a local bank account becomes useful for longer stays) and understand roughly what things cost, see our cost of living guide.
- **Health insurance.** Arrange cover appropriate for an extended stay, not just short-trip travel insurance.
- **Initial accommodation.** Book a shorter-term stay (a month, not a year) for your first weeks, giving yourself time to see areas in person before committing to a longer lease.
- **Belongings.** Decide what you're bringing versus shipping versus buying locally; see our guide on shipping belongings to Bali if you have more than fits in luggage.
- **Documents.** Passport with sufficient validity, any documents needed for your visa application, and copies (digital and physical) of everything important.

## First weeks in Bali: what to sort locally

- **Explore areas before committing long-term.** Spend time in Sanur, Canggu, Umalas and Ungasan (or wherever fits your priorities) before signing a 6–12 month lease; see our guide on the best place to live in Bali.
- **SIM card and local number**, useful for everything from ride-hailing apps to local admin.
- **Local bank account**, if you're staying long enough to justify it, useful for paying rent, bills and staff without repeated international transfer fees.
- **Get to know the practical side of your chosen area**: nearest hospital or clinic, grocery options, and typical traffic patterns at different times of day.
- **Sign a longer-term lease** only once you've confirmed area and villa fit; see our guide on what's included in a long-term villa rental before signing.

## A simple before/after table

| Stage | Focus |
|---|---|
| Before leaving | Visa, insurance, initial short-term accommodation, finances |
| First weeks | Area exploration, local SIM/bank, longer-term lease decision |
| Ongoing | Staff arrangements if wanted, settling routines, visa renewals if applicable |

## Why renting short-term first matters

Committing to a year-long villa lease before you've experienced daily life in a specific area is the single most common regret we hear about from people who moved to Bali. A month or two in a flexible, shorter-term villa costs more per month than a yearly lease, but it buys you real information about traffic patterns, noise, proximity to what you actually use, and whether the area suits you, before you're locked into a longer commitment.

## Common mistakes worth avoiding

Signing a long lease sight-unseen based only on photos, underestimating visa lead times and requirements, not budgeting realistically for the first month or two of higher costs while you're still finding your footing, and assuming one area of Bali represents the whole island, when the character of Sanur, Canggu, Umalas and Ungasan genuinely differs enough to matter.

## Building a rough timeline

It helps to think of the whole move in three loose phases rather than one long undifferentiated to-do list. Two to three months before departure is generally the window for starting visa applications and any schooling research if you're moving with children, since both can take longer than expected and benefit from an early start. Four to six weeks out is a reasonable point to book your initial short-term accommodation and sort health insurance, once your travel dates are firmer. The final two weeks are best kept for the practical logistics: confirming documents, packing, and deciding what's coming with you versus what you're shipping or buying locally once you land.

This isn't a rigid schedule, and plenty of people move successfully on shorter timelines, but spacing things out this way avoids the common trap of trying to sort visa, accommodation, insurance and shipping all in the final couple of weeks before flying, when each of those tends to take longer than expected once you're actually working through it.

## Keeping a physical and digital copy of key documents

Before you leave, scan and store digital copies of your passport, visa documents, insurance policy and any other paperwork you might need to reference or replace while abroad, keeping them somewhere accessible even if your physical originals are lost or your phone is unavailable (a cloud folder plus emailing copies to yourself both work). This is a small step that's easy to skip in the rush of packing, but it can save real time and stress if you ever need to replace a document or prove something to an official while you're already in Bali and away from home.

## Setting realistic expectations for the first month

Almost everyone who moves to Bali, however well prepared, finds the first month involves more small friction and adjustment than they expected: figuring out where to buy what, adjusting to different administrative processes, and simply learning a new place's rhythms. Going in with the expectation that the first month is genuinely a settling-in period, rather than expecting to feel fully at home immediately, makes the inevitable small frustrations easier to handle without them feeling like signs something's gone wrong with the move itself.

## A final sanity check before you book flights

Before you finalise dates, run through this quick sanity check: is your visa plan confirmed with a licensed agent, do you have a genuinely short-term, flexible first accommodation booked rather than a long lease sight-unseen, and have you set aside a realistic buffer above your expected ongoing budget for the first month or two. If all three are covered, you're in a strong position for the move itself, whatever smaller details still need sorting.

## FAQ

### Should I book a long-term villa lease before arriving in Bali?
Generally no, it's better to book a shorter-term stay for your first month and explore areas in person before committing to a longer lease. This avoids the common mistake of signing a year's lease based only on photos.

### What visa should I sort before moving to Bali?
This depends on your nationality, purpose and intended stay length; confirm current categories with a licensed visa agent before booking flights. See our guide on visas needed to rent a villa in Bali for the general framework.

### How much should I budget for my first month moving to Bali?
Budget more than your expected ongoing monthly cost, since flexible short-term accommodation costs more per month than a long-term lease, and you'll likely make some spending mistakes while still learning the local landscape.

### Do I need a local bank account when moving to Bali?
It becomes useful for longer stays, particularly for paying rent, bills and any staff without repeated international transfer fees, though international cards work for many day-to-day purchases in the meantime.

### What's the biggest mistake people make when moving to Bali?
Committing to a long-term villa lease before spending real time in the area, based only on photos and descriptions. A shorter initial stay to explore properly before signing anything longer avoids this.
