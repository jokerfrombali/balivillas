Cost of living in Bali per month depends mainly on accommodation choice and how much you eat out versus cook, more than on any single "average" figure that gets quoted online. A remote worker in a shared guesthouse eating mostly local warungs spends very differently from a family in a private villa eating at Western restaurants several times a week, and both are legitimately "living in Bali."

Rather than offering one misleading average, it's more useful to understand the main cost categories and what drives each one, so you can build a realistic budget for your own situation.

## The main cost categories

**Accommodation.** Usually the largest single line item, and the one with the widest range: a shared guesthouse room costs a fraction of a private pool villa. See our comparisons of monthly villa versus guesthouse and villa versus apartment for how these options typically stack up.

**Food.** Eating at local warungs is inexpensive; eating primarily at Western-style cafés and restaurants, common in Canggu and Seminyak especially, costs considerably more. Most long-term residents land somewhere in between, and groceries for cooking at home (easier if your villa has a full kitchen) can meaningfully lower this category.

**Transport.** A scooter is the standard mode of transport for most residents and is inexpensive to rent monthly and run; a car costs more to rent and adds parking and fuel considerations, though it's more practical for families or in the rain.

**Utilities.** Electricity (driven mainly by air conditioning use, see our electricity cost guide), water and internet. These are typically separate from villa rent; see our guide on hidden costs of long-term rental for the fuller picture.

**Health insurance and incidental medical costs.** Not always factored in by people budgeting for the first time, but worth including, especially for longer stays.

**Visa costs**, which vary by visa type and are a recurring or one-off cost depending on your visa category (see our guide on what visa you need to rent a villa in Bali).

## A framework for building your own estimate

| Category | Low end drivers | High end drivers |
|---|---|---|
| Accommodation | Shared room, guesthouse | Private pool villa, prime area |
| Food | Local warungs, cooking at home | Western restaurants, frequent dining out |
| Transport | Scooter | Car with driver |
| Utilities | Modest AC use, water included | Heavy AC use, everything metered |
| Health/visa | Basic cover | Comprehensive insurance, premium visa route |

Build your own number by picking a realistic point in each row for your actual habits, rather than adopting someone else's average, since the "cost of living in Bali" you'll see quoted online often reflects one specific lifestyle presented as universal.

## Why area changes the number

Canggu and Seminyak, with their concentration of Western-style cafés, co-working spaces and higher-end villas, tend to run more expensive than Sanur, Umalas or areas further from the main tourist strip, for comparable standards of accommodation and dining. This isn't a hard rule since specific villas and habits vary, but it's a reasonable starting assumption when comparing areas.

## Solo versus couple versus family

Costs don't simply multiply per person. A couple sharing a villa splits accommodation cost roughly in half per person compared to living separately, while food and transport scale more directly with headcount. See our guides on monthly budget for a couple and cost of living for a family for scenario-specific breakdowns.

## What most people underestimate

New arrivals commonly underbudget for: air conditioning-driven electricity costs in the wet or hot seasons, the cumulative cost of frequent Western dining even when individual meals seem cheap, and visa-related costs and time if they haven't planned that side properly in advance.

## How to stress-test your own estimate

Once you've built a first-pass budget from the framework above, it's worth stress-testing it against a couple of realistic scenarios rather than treating the number as fixed. Ask yourself what happens to the total if you eat out twice as often as planned in your first month, which is common while you're still learning where to shop and cook; what happens if your electricity bill lands at the higher end of the range because you're running air conditioning most of the day during a hot stretch; and what happens if an unplanned cost, a medical visit, a visa complication, or simply replacing something you didn't bring, shows up in month one. Building a buffer of roughly 10–15% above your baseline estimate for at least the first few months is a sensible habit, since almost everyone underestimates slightly when they're still new to a place.

## Why the first month rarely matches the ongoing average

Most people's first month in Bali costs more than their steady-state monthly average, for reasons that have nothing to do with poor planning. You're likely eating out more while you find your favourite spots, buying small household items you didn't think to bring or arrange in advance, and possibly paying for a flexible short-term stay rather than a cheaper long-term lease if you haven't yet committed to an area. Treat your first month's actual spending as a data point for calibrating your ongoing budget, not as a representative steady-state number, and revisit your estimate after six to eight weeks once your routines have settled.

## Tracking your spending once you've arrived

Once you're actually in Bali, a simple habit of tracking spending by category for the first two or three months, even roughly, is far more useful than any estimate built in advance, since it turns your budget from a guess into something based on real data specific to how you actually live here. Most people find one or two categories run higher than expected and one or two run lower, and knowing which is which lets you adjust deliberately rather than just feeling generally over or under budget without knowing why.

## A worked example of how two budgets can diverge

Consider two remote workers, both single, both renting a 1-bedroom villa in a similar area at a similar rent. One cooks most meals at home, rides a scooter, uses a prepaid electricity token and keeps AC use moderate, sticking mostly to local warungs when eating out. The other eats at Western cafés most days, runs AC throughout, and occasionally hires a car with driver for convenience. Despite identical accommodation costs, their total monthly spending can differ by a wide margin, illustrating why "cost of living in Bali" resists a single number: the accommodation line might be nearly the same, but food, transport and utilities diverge sharply based on daily choices rather than anything about where they live.

## Currency to budget in

Decide early whether you're thinking in your home currency or Indonesian rupiah for day-to-day budgeting, and be consistent, since flipping between the two casually makes it easy to lose track of your real spending, particularly if your home currency has moved meaningfully against the rupiah recently. Many long-term residents find it easier to think in rupiah for local spending (food, transport, utilities) once they're settled, while still tracking larger, less frequent costs like accommodation or annual visa fees in their home currency for comparison purposes.

## A final reminder about published averages

If you see a specific "cost of living in Bali" figure quoted confidently online, treat it as one person's or one source's specific lifestyle, not a fact about Bali itself. The framework in this guide, built from your own realistic habits across the main categories, will always be more useful to you than any single published number, however precise it looks.

## FAQ

### What is a realistic monthly budget for living in Bali?
It varies enormously by lifestyle, mainly accommodation standard and dining habits, so there's no single reliable figure. Build an estimate from the main categories, accommodation, food, transport and utilities, based on your own realistic habits.

### Is accommodation the biggest cost of living in Bali?
For most people, yes, it's typically the largest single category, and also the one with the widest range between a shared room and a private villa. It's worth budgeting carefully since it affects your total more than any other single choice.

### Does eating out a lot significantly increase the cost of living in Bali?
Yes, dining regularly at Western-style cafés and restaurants, especially in areas like Canggu and Seminyak, adds up considerably more than eating at local warungs or cooking at home in a villa with a full kitchen.

### Is Bali cheaper to live in than living in a Western country?
For many categories, yes, though it depends heavily on lifestyle choices, and some habits (imported goods, Western dining, premium villas) can bring costs closer to home-country levels. See our guide on whether Bali is cheap to live in for a fuller comparison.

### How much does transport cost per month living in Bali?
A scooter is the standard, low-cost option most residents use; a car costs more to rent and run but suits families or those who prefer not to ride. Choose based on your household size and comfort with scooter travel.
