Supermarkets in Bali cover most everyday needs well, and long-stayers typically end up shopping across two or three different types of store rather than relying on just one, because no single place covers everything at a reasonable price. Large general supermarkets handle packaged goods, household items and a decent range of produce. Traditional local markets (pasar) are usually cheaper and fresher for fruit, vegetables and fish. Specialist import stores fill the gap for specific ingredients from home that neither of the other two reliably stocks.

Building a weekly routine around this split, rather than expecting one shop to do everything, is what most settled long-stayers end up doing, and it is worth planning from your first week rather than discovering it gradually.

## The three main shopping channels

| Channel | Best for | Typical trade-off |
|---|---|---|
| Large supermarket chains | Packaged goods, household items, reliable stock | Produce sometimes less fresh, pricier than local market |
| Traditional local markets (pasar) | Fresh produce, fish, cheaper prices | Requires some bargaining norms, less predictable stock |
| Specialist import stores | Imported goods, specific ingredients from home | More expensive, not always in every area |

Most long-stayers develop a rhythm: a weekly or twice-weekly trip to a local market for fresh produce, a supermarket run for staples and household items, and an occasional visit to an import store for something specific. Delivery apps covering supermarket goods are also widely used in the main expat areas, which cuts down on trips for routine top-ups.

## How availability differs by area

Canggu and Umalas have the widest range of supermarkets and specialist stores given the density of the expat population there. Sanur has solid general supermarket coverage and a well-established local market, suiting a family or long-stay routine without needing to travel far. Ungasan, being more spread out on the Bukit peninsula, generally means a longer drive for the widest selection, so factor this into your routine if you are staying there.

## Budgeting a realistic weekly shop

Costs vary considerably depending on how much of your shop leans toward imported goods versus local produce, and by household size and cooking habits. Rather than quoting a figure likely to be outdated quickly, the practical approach is to track your own first two or three weeks of spending across the different channels, then adjust the mix toward local markets and general supermarkets if cost is a priority, or toward specialist stores if specific ingredients matter more than budget.

- Start with a local market trip in your first week to learn what is genuinely fresh and well-priced nearby
- Use a general supermarket for household and packaged staples rather than produce where possible
- Reserve specialist import store trips for genuinely hard-to-find items, since markup there is usually higher
- Try a grocery delivery app for routine top-ups once you know your regular list

## Cooking at home versus eating out

Bali's food scene makes eating out genuinely affordable compared with many home countries, which shifts the calculation for some long-stayers away from heavy home cooking toward a mixed routine of cooking some meals and eating out or ordering in for others. Our villas all include fully equipped kitchens, so the choice is entirely about preference and budget rather than facility limitations.

## Household staff and grocery routines

Some long-stayers, particularly families on a longer stay, employ household help who also handle regular grocery shopping and know the best local markets and prices better than a newcomer would. If this is something you are considering, it is worth discussing with your villa host or property manager, since local staffing norms and expectations differ from what many international renters are used to.

## Packaging, plastic and bringing your own bags

Most Bali supermarkets still default to plastic bags at checkout, though a number of chains now charge a small fee per bag or offer paper alternatives following periodic local packaging rules. Bringing a reusable bag or two on your regular shop is a small habit worth picking up early, both to avoid the recurring small charge and because bin collection and recycling infrastructure in most areas is less consistent than many long-stayers are used to at home, so reducing what you bring in helps. Bottled water is also worth planning for specifically: most long-stayers buy large refillable water gallons (galon) rather than single-use bottles for everyday drinking, since tap water is not suitable to drink directly, and a gallon delivery or exchange service is available through most local shops and some supermarkets.

## FAQ

### Are there large supermarket chains in Bali, or only local markets?
Both. Large general supermarket chains operate across the main areas and cover packaged goods, household items and a reasonable produce range. Traditional local markets exist alongside them and are usually the better, cheaper option specifically for fresh produce and fish.

### Which area of Bali has the best grocery shopping options?
Canggu and Umalas have the widest range given the density of expat-focused stores there. Sanur has solid general coverage and an established local market. Ungasan, being more spread out, usually means a longer drive for the widest selection.

### Can I find imported or Western food products in Bali?
Yes, specialist import stores in the main expat areas stock a range of imported goods, though prices are generally higher than local products and availability is not always consistent for every specific item. Check a couple of stores if you need something specific.

### Is grocery delivery available in Bali?
Yes, delivery apps covering supermarket goods operate widely in the main expat areas, and many long-stayers use them for routine top-ups between larger in-person shopping trips.

### Is it cheaper to cook at home or eat out in Bali?
Both are viable, and Bali's food scene makes eating out genuinely affordable compared with many home countries, which is why many long-stayers mix cooking at home with eating out rather than committing fully to either approach.
