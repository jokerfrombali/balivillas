Meeting people in Bali as an expat works best through recurring, structured activities rather than relying on chance encounters. Coworking spaces, sports groups, language exchanges and recurring social events give you repeated contact with the same people, which is what actually builds a social circle, more reliably than a single party or a one-off tourist experience.

Living in Bali as an expat for more than a few weeks makes this a genuinely practical question, not just a nice-to-have, since the areas that suit daily tourist visits are not always where the more settled long-stay community actually spends its time.

## Why recurring activities beat one-off events

A single event, however well attended, rarely produces lasting friendships on its own, because you meet a lot of people briefly and see almost none of them again. A weekly activity, whether that is a run club, a coworking membership, a language class or a regular beach volleyball session, puts you in front of the same group repeatedly, which is what actually turns "met once" into "know reasonably well."

If you are moving to Bali as an expat and only have a few months, prioritise finding two or three recurring activities early rather than spreading yourself across many one-off events in your first weeks. It is a more efficient use of limited time and produces deeper connections faster.

## Coworking spaces as a social entry point

For remote workers, a coworking membership is often the single most efficient way to meet other long-stayers, because it combines a daily routine with proximity to people in a similar life situation. Umalas and Canggu in particular have a concentration of coworking spaces and the cafés that cluster around them, which is part of why these areas suit remote workers specifically; our guide to living in Umalas covers this in more detail.

Even if you do not need a coworking desk for the work itself, some long-stayers pay for a membership primarily for the social structure it provides, which is a reasonable trade-off if meeting people is a genuine priority for your stay.

## Sports, fitness and hobby groups

Recurring sports and fitness activities, from surf lessons to yoga classes to running groups, tend to attract a mix of long-stay expats and locals and provide the same repeated-contact benefit as coworking. These groups often organise informally through messaging apps or social media once you find the first one, so the initial barrier is finding one group, after which further connections tend to follow more easily.

| Activity type | Where it tends to concentrate | Why it works for meeting people |
|---|---|---|

## Language and cultural bridges

Learning even basic Indonesian, covered in more depth in our separate guide on language basics for living in Bali, opens up interactions with the local community that purely expat-focused socialising does not reach. This is not necessary for meeting other expats, but it broadens the social circle beyond a purely foreign bubble, which many long-stayers find adds real depth to a longer stay.

Language exchange meetups specifically are a structured, low-pressure way to meet both other foreigners practising Indonesian and locals practising English, and tend to run on a recurring schedule in the areas with a larger long-stay population.

## Family-specific considerations

For families, the social entry points differ from the single or couple long-stayer pattern. School communities, if children are enrolled locally, and family-oriented recurring activities tend to be the more natural route, and Sanur in particular has a reputation among long-stayers as suiting families for exactly this reason, alongside its quieter beachside setting. Our guide to living in Sanur covers the area's character in more detail for families specifically.

## What tends not to work as well

Relying solely on nightlife-oriented socialising in Canggu's beach club scene can produce a lot of surface-level contact without much depth, since much of that crowd is on shorter stays and turns over quickly. This is not a criticism of the scene itself, simply a practical note that if your goal is a lasting social circle for a long stay, pairing any nightlife socialising with at least one or two recurring, lower-key activities tends to work better.

Similarly, waiting passively for connections to happen through your accommodation or neighbourhood alone, without actively joining anything, is slower than deliberately seeking out a recurring group in your first few weeks.

## Building a routine that supports this

Practically, the fastest way to build a social circle when moving to Bali as an expat is to commit to two or three recurring things in your first month: perhaps a coworking membership, one sports or fitness class, and one language or hobby meetup. This gives you enough repeated contact across different groups to build a genuine circle within a few months, rather than staying on the surface of a large, transient expat population.

## FAQ

### What is the fastest way to start meeting people in Bali as an expat?
Join a recurring activity in your first weeks, such as a coworking space, a sports or fitness class, or a language exchange meetup, rather than relying on one-off events. Repeated contact with the same people is what builds friendships, not a single well-attended party.

### Is Canggu or Umalas better for meeting other expats?
Both have strong expat communities, but the character differs: Canggu leans toward surf, beach clubs and a faster social pace, while Umalas leans toward a quieter, coworking and café-centred crowd. Choose based on which pace suits your stay rather than assuming one is objectively better for socialising.

### Is it harder to meet people if I am moving to Bali as an expat with a family?
The entry points differ rather than being harder: school communities and family-oriented recurring activities tend to work better than nightlife-focused socialising. Sanur in particular has a reputation among long-stayers as suiting families for this reason.

### Do I need to learn Indonesian to meet people in Bali as an expat?
No, plenty of socialising happens in English within the expat community, but learning basic Indonesian opens up interactions with the local community and adds depth many long-stayers value. It is not a prerequisite, just an additional avenue.

### How long does it typically take to build a social circle when living in Bali as an expat?
This varies by person and by how many recurring activities you commit to, but joining two or three regular groups in your first month tends to produce a genuine circle within a few months, compared with relying only on chance encounters. Consistency matters more than the number of events attended.
