Bali villa holding costs are everything you pay after the purchase closes to keep the property running: annual land and building tax (PBB), staff wages if you employ your own, routine maintenance for a pool, garden and tropical-climate building, utilities, insurance, and management fees if you use a company to handle guests and upkeep on your behalf. None of these are optional line items you can skip for long without the villa's condition or rentability suffering.

Buyers who focus only on the purchase price and permit costs often underestimate what a villa actually costs to run well for years, and the gap between "we bought a villa" and "we own a well-maintained, rentable villa" is largely this ongoing cost stack.

## The main components of holding costs

| Cost category | What it covers | Frequency |
|---|---|---|
| PBB (land and building tax) | Annual government tax on the property | Yearly |
| Staff (housekeeping, gardener, security, pool maintenance) | Day-to-day upkeep | Ongoing, typically monthly wages |
| Utilities | Electricity, water, internet | Monthly |
| Maintenance and repairs | Pool equipment, AC servicing, general wear | Ongoing, budget as a reserve |
| Insurance | Property and liability cover | Annual premium |
| Management fee, if using a company | Marketing, guest care, oversight | Percentage of bookings, if applicable |

## Why the tropical climate drives cost up

Bali's climate is hard on buildings and pools in ways that owners coming from temperate climates often underestimate. Humidity, salt air in coastal areas, and heavy seasonal rain accelerate wear on paintwork, wood, metal fittings and pool equipment. A villa left unmaintained for even a few months can visibly deteriorate, with mould, rust and garden overgrowth setting in faster than most owners expect. Budgeting maintenance as a routine, ongoing cost rather than an occasional expense is the difference between a villa that holds its condition and one that needs a costly catch-up renovation every few years.

## Staffing: employ directly or use a management company

Owners generally choose between hiring staff directly (housekeeper, gardener, pool technician, security) or engaging a management company that handles staffing as part of a broader service. Direct hiring gives more control but adds employer obligations and requires someone to actually manage the staff day to day, which is difficult from abroad. A management company folds staffing into its service, which is typically reflected in its fee structure rather than a separate line item you manage yourself. ## A practical way to budget holding costs

1. Get a realistic maintenance reserve estimate based on the villa's size, age and finish, not a generic percentage
2. Confirm the current PBB assessment on the specific property, not an estimate from a different villa
3. Decide early whether you are staffing directly or using a management company, since this changes your monthly cash flow shape significantly
4. Budget utilities based on actual usage patterns if the villa is occupied by guests versus left empty for long stretches
5. Set aside a separate reserve for larger periodic costs: roof work, repainting, pool resurfacing, that do not happen every year but do happen eventually

## Empty villa versus rented villa: different cost profiles

A villa sitting empty for long periods still incurs most holding costs, PBB, base utilities, security, basic maintenance, without the offsetting income a rented villa can generate. This is one reason many owners choose to rent their villa, whether short-term or long-term, even if income was not the primary motivation for buying: it helps offset a cost stack that continues regardless of occupancy. ## Comparing holding costs across the four areas we operate in

Holding costs vary somewhat by area, driven mainly by land and building size, proximity to the coast (which affects maintenance intensity), and local staffing availability. Sanur, Canggu, Umalas and Ungasan each have slightly different profiles: Sanur's calmer beachside setting and larger villa stock, Canggu's beachfront exposure adding to maintenance demands, Umalas's quieter inland lanes, and Ungasan's clifftop position on the Bukit. None of these differences are large enough to be the deciding factor on their own, but they are worth asking about specifically for any villa you are considering.

## What goes wrong when buyers underestimate this

- **Budgeting maintenance as an occasional expense rather than a routine one.** A villa that gets a "big tidy-up" once a year, rather than continuous upkeep, tends to need more expensive fixes more often, because small problems (a leaking pipe, early rust on a railing) are caught late.
- **Hiring staff directly without a clear plan for oversight while living abroad.** Direct staffing gives control, but only if someone is actually checking in regularly; an absent owner with no local oversight structure often ends up with a villa that looks fine in photos and is quietly deteriorating.
- **Treating a management company's fee as pure cost, without weighing it against what direct staffing and oversight would actually cost in time and risk.** The fair comparison is total cost and outcome, not just the headline percentage.
- **Not budgeting for large, infrequent costs** such as repainting, pool resurfacing or roof work, which do not happen every year but are certain to happen eventually, and can be a shock if there is no reserve set aside.

## A decision framework: self-staff or use a management company

| Factor | Leans toward direct staffing | Leans toward a management company |
|---|---|---|
| You live in Bali or visit very frequently | Yes | Less critical either way |
| You want to actively manage guest bookings and marketing yourself | Yes | No, this is exactly what a management fee covers |
| You value predictable, bundled costs over granular control | No | Yes |
| The villa will sit empty for long stretches without you nearby | No, oversight risk is high | Yes |
| You want monthly reporting and a single point of accountability | Depends on your own systems | Yes, typically built into the service |

Neither option is universally right; it depends on how much time, distance and interest you personally have in the day-to-day running of the property.

## FAQ

### What is the single biggest ongoing cost of owning a villa in Bali?
For most owners, it is staffing and maintenance combined, since a villa with a pool and tropical garden needs regular, skilled attention to stay in good condition. PBB (annual tax) is usually smaller by comparison, though it should still be budgeted precisely rather than estimated.

### Can I reduce holding costs by using the villa myself instead of renting it?
Using it yourself avoids the operational complexity of guest turnover, but the underlying holding costs, tax, staff, maintenance, utilities, continue regardless of whether the villa is occupied by guests, by you, or empty. Renting it out, even occasionally, can help offset these costs.

### Does a management company reduce my total holding costs?
A management company charges a fee, typically a commission on bookings, but folds staffing coordination, guest care and marketing into that service. Whether it reduces your net cost depends on how it compares to what you would otherwise spend managing everything yourself, including the value of your own time.

### How much should I set aside for unexpected repairs?
There is no fixed industry figure we can quote reliably, but a sensible approach is a dedicated maintenance reserve built up over time rather than treating repairs as a surprise each time they occur. Ask a local property manager or contractor for a realistic estimate based on the specific villa's age and condition.

### Do holding costs differ much between Sanur, Canggu, Umalas and Ungasan?
There are some differences driven by proximity to the coast, villa size and local staffing, but the main drivers of holding cost, the villa's size, condition and whether it is staffed or managed, matter more than the area alone. Ask for specifics on any villa you are comparing across areas.
