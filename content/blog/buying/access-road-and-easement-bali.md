Land access in Bali needs to be legally documented, not just physically usable, and the difference matters enormously once you try to get a building permit or, later, sell the property. A plot that's currently reached by an informal path across a neighbour's field, with no registered easement or access agreement, is a real risk: the neighbour can restrict or block it, and a permitting authority may not accept it as valid access for a building application. This is a particularly common issue on land away from established main roads — exactly the kind of quieter, greener plots that often look most appealing for a private villa. The appeal and the risk tend to come from the same underlying feature: distance from a formal, already-serviced road.

## Types of access and what they mean

**Direct frontage on a public road.** The cleanest situation — the plot borders a registered public road directly, with no dependency on anyone else's land.

**Registered right-of-way / easement.** An access route across another party's land that's formally documented and registered, giving you a legal, enforceable right to use it. This is workable and common, but only if it's actually registered, not just agreed verbally with the current neighbour.

**Informal or customary access.** A path used by convention or the current landowner's goodwill, with no registered legal right attached. This is the risky category — access can be restricted, blocked, or disputed, particularly if the neighbouring land changes hands.

| Access type | Security level | What to verify |
|---|---|---|
| Direct road frontage | High | Confirm the road is a registered public road, not a private track |
| Registered easement/right-of-way | Moderate to high | Confirm it's formally registered, not just a verbal agreement, and check its width and any restrictions |
| Informal/customary access | Low | Treat as a red flag; negotiate formal registration before purchase or walk away |

## Why access affects your building permit, not just convenience

Building permit applications typically require documented legal access to a public road as part of the application. Land with only informal access can face permit delays or refusal, independent of everything else about the plot being suitable. This isn't a minor administrative point — it can be the difference between a buildable plot and one that sits undeveloped because the permit can't be completed. Confirming this before signing avoids discovering it after you already own land you can't build on.

## How to verify access before buying

Ask your notary or independent lawyer to confirm, in writing, whether the plot has documented public road frontage or a registered easement, and to show you the specific document, not just describe it. If access relies on an informal arrangement, this should be a condition of purchase, not an assumption: either the seller formalises and registers the access before the deal closes, or the price should reflect the real risk and cost of doing so yourself afterward, if it's even achievable.

## What formalising access typically involves

If access needs to be formalised, this usually means negotiating a registered easement agreement with the landowner whose property the access crosses, which is then recorded with the relevant land office. This can take time and, in some cases, negotiation leverage or payment to the neighbouring landowner, particularly if they have no strong reason to agree quickly. This is a cost and delay worth pricing into any deal where access isn't already secured.

## Access, utilities and site services often travel together

Access routes frequently double as the path for utility connections — water and electricity lines commonly run along the same access route to a plot. If access is informal or contested, utility connection can face similar complications. See our guide on [connecting water and electricity to new land](/blog/buying/water-and-electricity-connection-new-land/) for how these two issues typically interact on undeveloped plots.

## A checklist before committing to a plot's access

1. Confirm in writing whether the plot has direct public road frontage or relies on an easement/right-of-way.
2. If an easement is claimed, verify it's formally registered with the land office, not just a verbal or informal arrangement.
3. Check the registered width and any restrictions on the easement (vehicle access, hours, shared use).
4. Ask what happens to the easement if the land it crosses is sold to a new owner.
5. If access is informal, make formal registration a condition of the purchase agreement, with cost allocated clearly.
6. Confirm the access route is also suitable for utility connection, not just vehicle access.

## Why this matters more on quieter, greener plots

The plots most likely to have access issues are often the ones that look most attractive on a site visit — set back from the main road, surrounded by rice fields or gardens, quiet and private. That's exactly the profile where informal access across neighbouring land is most common, so it's worth being more diligent, not less, on land that appeals for its seclusion. ## FAQ

### What happens if land access in Bali is only informal?

The neighbouring landowner can, in principle, restrict or block that access, and a permitting authority may not accept informal access as sufficient for a building permit application. Treat informal access as a serious risk requiring formal registration before or as a condition of purchase, not something to accept and hope continues.

### How do I check if land access is legally registered in Bali?

Ask your notary or independent lawyer to confirm and show you the specific registered document (title or easement registration with the land office), rather than relying on a verbal description from the seller or agent. This should be a standard part of pre-purchase due diligence.

### Can I negotiate an easement after I've already bought the land?

You can try, but you'll have much less leverage as the new owner needing access than the seller would have had before the deal closed. It's far safer to make formal access registration a condition of the purchase agreement, negotiated before you commit to buying.

### Does access affect anything besides getting to the property?

Yes — building permit applications typically require documented legal road access, and access routes often carry utility connections as well. Informal or contested access can complicate both permitting and utility connection, not just day-to-day convenience.

### Are access issues more common on certain types of land?

Yes, plots set back from main roads, surrounded by rice fields or gardens, or reached via paths across neighbouring land are the most common cases. These are often the most visually appealing, quiet plots, which is exactly why extra diligence on access matters most there.
