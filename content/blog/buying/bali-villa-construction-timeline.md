A villa build in Bali runs through five broad stages — due diligence and land purchase, design, permitting, construction, and finishing/handover — and each one has its own typical duration and its own way of running late. For a simple one- or two-bedroom villa, 10-18 months from signed land deal to keys-in-hand is a realistic indicative range; larger or more complex villas, or anything requiring extensive site works, can run well beyond that.

The two stages buyers most often underestimate are permitting and the rainy season's effect on construction speed. Both are predictable if you plan for them, and both are the two places a fixed completion date quoted verbally by a contractor tends to slip.

## The five stages and what happens in each

**1. Due diligence and land purchase (4-12 weeks).** Title checks, zoning verification, notary work and signing. This stage's length depends heavily on how clean the land title is and how quickly the seller responds; see our guide on due diligence when buying from a developer for what this actually involves.

**2. Design (6-12 weeks for concept through construction drawings).** Concept design, revisions, then detailed construction drawings the builder will actually work from. This overlaps with permitting in a well-run project, since permit drawings are often a subset of the full construction set.

**3. Permitting (8-20 weeks, sometimes longer).** Building permits (commonly referred to under the PBG system that replaced the older IMB process) plus any local village (desa) approvals. This is the widest range in the whole timeline and the stage most likely to blow a budget-conscious schedule, because it depends on local government processing speed, which varies by regency and can't be reliably sped up by paying more. **4. Construction (6-12 months).** Foundations, structure, roof, walls, then interior fit-out. Rainy season (roughly November to March) slows exterior and foundation work in particular; a build that starts structural work in October will likely move faster than one starting in December.

**5. Finishing and handover (4-8 weeks).** Final fit-out, landscaping, utility connections, snagging list, and sign-off. This is also when you'd typically arrange management if you plan to rent the villa out — see our guide on [villa management fees](/blog/owners/villa-management-fees-bali/) for how that conversation usually starts.

## A timeline overview

| Stage | Typical duration | Main risk to the schedule |
|---|---|---|
| Due diligence & purchase | 4-12 weeks | Title issues, slow seller responses |
| Design | 6-12 weeks | Client-driven revisions |
| Permitting | 8-20 weeks | Local government processing speed |
| Construction | 6-12 months | Rainy season, contractor capacity, material delays |
| Finishing & handover | 4-8 weeks | Snagging, utility connection delays |

These ranges are indicative and vary significantly by villa size, site conditions (slope, access, soil) and how decisive the buyer is during design. Treat the total as a planning range, not a promise, and build in contingency time as well as contingency budget.

## Why permits are the hardest stage to predict

Unlike design or construction, which move roughly at the pace you and your builder set, permitting depends on a local government office's queue and, in practice, on how complete and correctly prepared your application is the first time. An incomplete submission that gets sent back for corrections can add weeks. This is one reason a good architect or a notary experienced in the specific regency matters more than buyers expect — local knowledge of what a particular office wants to see shortens this stage more reliably than urgency does.

Because permitting timing is genuinely uncertain, we'd treat any contractor who promises a fixed total completion date before permits are even submitted with some scepticism. A realistic contractor gives you a range for permitting and a firmer number for construction once permits are in hand.

## What actually causes delays in practice

Beyond permits and rain, the patterns we see repeatedly are: change orders during construction (client-requested design changes after the build has started, which usually cost both time and money); material delivery delays, particularly for imported fittings; contractor overcommitment, where a builder running several projects at once quietly deprioritises yours; and access issues on tight or sloped sites that weren't fully scoped before signing the build contract.

A written schedule with milestone dates, tied to payment releases, is your main tool for keeping a build on track. If the contractor won't commit to a milestone schedule with dates, that's worth treating as a signal, not just an inconvenience.

## How the timeline compares to buying an existing villa

If the timeline itself is the deciding factor, buying a completed, already-managed villa is the direct alternative to building. It removes the design, permit and construction stages entirely — you're looking at due diligence and a purchase transaction only, which can close in weeks rather than over a year. ## Steps to protect your own timeline

- Get a written, milestone-based construction schedule from the contractor before signing, not a verbal estimate.
- Start the permit application as early as design allows rather than waiting for final construction drawings if the process allows staged submission.
- Time major structural work to start before the rainy season where possible.
- Build in a written contingency period (we'd suggest at least 4-8 weeks) for the finishing and handover stage specifically, since this is where snagging issues surface.
- Ask the contractor how many other projects they're running concurrently.

## FAQ

### How long does it take to build a villa in Bali from start to finish?

For a straightforward one- to two-bedroom villa, a realistic indicative range is 10-18 months from signed land purchase to handover, covering due diligence, design, permits, construction and finishing. Larger villas or complex sites take longer; always get a written milestone schedule from your specific contractor rather than relying on general ranges.

### What causes the biggest delays in a Bali villa build?

Permitting timing is the least predictable stage, since it depends on local government processing speed rather than your builder's pace. Rainy season (roughly November to March) is the second major factor, slowing exterior and foundation work in particular.

### Can I speed up the building permit process in Bali?

Not reliably by paying more; the main lever is submitting a complete, correctly prepared application the first time, which is where an architect or notary experienced with the specific local office adds real value. Rushing an incomplete submission usually adds delay rather than removing it. (as of 2026, verify)

### Should I start construction in the rainy or dry season?

Where you have a choice, starting foundation and structural work before the rainy season (roughly November to March) generally keeps the schedule moving faster than starting mid-rainy-season. This isn't a hard rule and depends on the specific site and drainage.

### Is buying an already-built villa faster than building one?

Yes, significantly. Buying a completed villa skips the design, permit and construction stages entirely, reducing the process to due diligence and a purchase transaction, which can close in weeks. It also gives you a known, inspectable property rather than a set of drawings and promises.
