Buying a villa without permits usually happens quietly: the price looks good, the villa is finished and occupied, and the missing PBG or SLF only surfaces when you ask directly or when your notary runs due diligence. It is not automatically a dealbreaker, but it changes the transaction from a straightforward purchase into one where you are pricing in someone else's unfinished paperwork.

The core question is not "is it illegal to buy" but "what does it cost me later, and can it be fixed." Some gaps are fixable through retroactive permitting; others point to deeper problems like zoning that will never support what has been built.

## What "no permits" usually means in practice

Villas without full permits generally fall into one of a few categories, and they carry very different levels of risk.

| Situation | Typical risk level |
|---|---|
| Built before current permit rules tightened, otherwise compliant | Lower; retroactive permitting is often straightforward |
| PBG obtained, but built differently from the approved plan | Medium; may need a revised permit and inspection |
| No PBG at all, land use technically zoned correctly | Medium to high; permitting possible but time-consuming |
| Land not zoned for the built function (e.g. villa on agricultural land) | High; may not be fixable without rezoning, which is not guaranteed |

The last category is the one that catches buyers out most often. A villa can look finished and rented successfully for years while sitting on land whose zoning does not support tourism accommodation. That is a structural problem, not a paperwork delay.

## Why sellers sometimes skip permits

Permitting takes time and money, and in the years when enforcement was lighter in some areas, some owners simply built and rented without completing the full process. This does not mean every unpermitted villa is a scam; many owners intend to formalise later, or inherited the gap from a previous owner, or genuinely did not understand the requirement. Understanding the seller's story matters less than verifying the current legal status yourself.

## What can go wrong after you buy

- Difficulty obtaining a formal short-term rental licence, which can limit how you legally operate the villa
- Inability to get a clean SLF, which then affects resale value and buyer confidence when you sell
- Exposure if local authorities increase enforcement in that area, which has happened in specific zones in Bali in recent years
- Lower resale price, because the next buyer's due diligence will surface the same gap and they will price it in too
- Financing and insurance complications, since some insurers and lenders ask for permit documentation

None of these mean the villa is worthless. They mean the discount you are getting on the purchase price needs to genuinely cover the cost and risk of resolving the gap, not just look like a bargain on the surface.

## How to check before you commit

1. Ask for the PKKPR (land use conformity), PBG (building permit) and SLF (function certificate) as documents, not verbal assurances
2. Have your notary or PPAT verify each one against local government records independently
3. If any is missing, ask specifically which one, why, and what would be required to obtain it now
4. Get a written cost and time estimate for closing the gap from a licensed consultant, not the seller's own estimate
5. Confirm the underlying land zoning supports the villa's actual use, since this is the one gap that sometimes cannot be fixed

## Negotiating around a permit gap

If due diligence turns up a fixable gap, there are a few honest ways to structure the deal: reduce the price by the verified cost of obtaining the missing permits plus a margin for delay, make permit completion a condition precedent to closing with the seller responsible, or hold part of the purchase price in escrow until the permits are confirmed. What does not work well is accepting a verbal promise that "it will be sorted" with no timeline or accountability attached, because once you have paid and taken possession, your leverage to make that happen drops sharply.

If the gap traces back to zoning rather than paperwork, walk away or bring in a specialist who can confirm rezoning is realistic before you commit any money.

## FAQ

### Is it illegal to buy a villa in Bali without permits?
Buying itself is not illegal, but operating a villa without the required permits for its actual use (particularly as short-term rental accommodation) can expose the owner to enforcement action, fines or closure depending on the area and current rules. The legal exposure sits with ongoing use, not the purchase transaction itself.

### Can missing permits always be fixed after purchase?
Not always. If the underlying land zoning does not support the villa's built function, no amount of paperwork after the fact resolves that, and rezoning is not guaranteed. If zoning is correct and only the building-level permits (PBG, SLF) are missing, retroactive permitting is usually possible, though it takes time and cost.

### How much should I discount the price for a permit gap?
There is no fixed formula; get a written quote from a licensed consultant for the actual cost and time to obtain the missing permits, then negotiate from that figure plus a margin for delay and uncertainty, not a guess.

### Who should verify permit status, me or the seller's agent?
Use your own notary or PPAT, independent of the seller's agent, to verify PKKPR, PBG and SLF against local government records. An agent working for the seller has an incentive to move the deal forward, not to flag problems.

### Does a villa's rental track record prove it is properly permitted?
No. A villa can be rented successfully for years without full permits simply because enforcement had not caught up with it yet. Rental history tells you about demand, not legal status; verify permits separately.
